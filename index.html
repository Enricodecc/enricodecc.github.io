Working papers

“When Is Prosocial Behavior Intuitive? The Moderating Role of Empathic Perspective Taking”

with Francesca Pancotto, Barbara Luppi, and Simone Righi
Under review
Abstract / project description +

This paper examines whether empathic perspective taking shapes the relationship between deliberation and prosocial choice using a laboratory experiment with 652 participants. Prosocial behavior is measured through a four-player Distribution Game and combined with measures of perspective taking, cognitive reflection, and response time. Perspective taking significantly moderates the association between response time and prosociality: at relatively high levels of perspective taking, longer deliberation is associated with lower prosociality, while no comparable moderation emerges for Cognitive Reflection Test performance. The results highlight the distinction between decision-specific deliberation and domain-general analytic reflection in explaining when prosocial behavior is intuitive.

“Corruption Perceptions and Revealed Behavior: Evidence from a Laboratory Experiment”

with Francesca Pancotto
Abstract / project description +

This paper examines whether general perceptions of corruption correspond to individuals' own behavior and experience in a laboratory corruption setting. Using data from 396 participants interacting repeatedly in fixed groups of three citizens and one judge, we compare stated corruption perceptions with multiple dimensions of behavior revealed under incentives. Among citizens, perceived corruption shows little systematic association with their own corrupt behavior or direct experience. Among judges, by contrast, perceptions are more closely related to role-specific behavior: higher bribe acceptance and punishment are associated with higher perceived corruption, while rejecting a larger share of positive bribe offers is associated with lower perceived corruption. The results suggest that the behavioral content of corruption perceptions may depend on the position from which corruption is experienced and observed.

“Validating LLM-Generated Synthetic Survey Samples: A Multi-Dimensional Framework for Social-Science Applications”

with Fulvio Castellacci and Arash Ahmadi
Under review
Abstract / project description +

This paper develops a multi-dimensional framework for assessing the empirical usefulness of LLM-generated synthetic survey samples, distinguishing predictive, distributional, and substantive validity. We apply the framework to Special Eurobarometer data on attitudes toward artificial intelligence and compare synthetic responses generated by four off-the-shelf LLMs with observed survey outcomes and conventional supervised machine-learning benchmarks. While LLM-generated samples are weaker at respondent-level prediction, some preserve more marginal variation and recover meaningful demographic, cross-country, and predictor-level structure. The results show that synthetic samples should be evaluated according to the empirical properties required by their intended use rather than through a single notion of accuracy.

“AI: Optimist or Skeptic? The Role of Trust in EU Institutions in Shaping Attitudes Toward AI”

with Fulvio Castellacci
Under review
Abstract / project description +

This paper investigates the determinants of European citizens' attitudes toward artificial intelligence, focusing on the role of trust in EU institutions. Using microdata from the Special Eurobarometer on Artificial Intelligence and the future of work, we combine econometric analysis with machine-learning methods to assess the relative importance of institutional trust alongside a broad set of individual characteristics. Trust in the EU emerges as a particularly strong predictor of attitudes toward AI, while more favorable views of AI coexist with stronger support for its regulation. The results point to a pattern of conditional optimism and suggest that confidence in regulatory institutions may play an important role in shaping public acceptance of artificial intelligence.

“Academic Familism and the Gelmini Reform: Evidence from Italian Universities (2001–2023)”

with Fabrizio Patriarca, Barbara Luppi, and Edoardo Santoni
Abstract / project description +

This paper investigates the persistence of academic familism in the Italian university system and the effects of the 2010 Gelmini Reform, which introduced explicit restrictions on the recruitment of relatives within universities. Using a longitudinal dataset covering Italian academic personnel from 2001 to 2023, we construct a proxy for familial concentration based on the intertemporal persistence of surnames within universities. The empirical analysis exploits the introduction of the reform to examine changes in the incidence and evolution of surname concentration over time, using an event-study framework together with alternative pre–post and regression-discontinuity-in-time specifications. The study contributes to the analysis of academic recruitment, meritocracy, and the effectiveness of institutional reforms aimed at limiting familism in higher education.

Research projects

Ongoing projects and research agendas, including studies in development.

“Digital Skills and Attitudes toward Artificial Intelligence in the European Labour Market”

Abstract / project description +

This project investigates how digital skills shape attitudes toward artificial intelligence in the European labour market. Using harmonized cross-country microdata from the Special Eurobarometer on Artificial Intelligence and the future of work, the analysis distinguishes between general evaluations of AI, attitudes toward its use and effects in the workplace, and preferences regarding regulation. The project examines whether higher digital skills are associated with systematically different views of AI and whether these relationships vary across socio-demographic characteristics, labour-market positions, and levels of institutional trust. The empirical strategy combines econometric analysis with flexible data-driven methods to capture non-linearities and heterogeneous relationships across individuals and countries.

“Who Is Judging Me? Gender Norms under Human and AI Evaluation”

Abstract / project description +

This project develops an experimental design to study whether gender differences in confidence signaling depend on whether individuals expect to be evaluated by a human or by an artificial-intelligence system. The proposed framework distinguishes actual performance, private beliefs about performance, and publicly communicated confidence, allowing the analysis to separate underlying beliefs from strategic self-presentation. The design crosses evaluator type (Human versus AI) with the observability of the worker's gender and aims to test whether male and female participants translate comparable beliefs and performance into different public signals depending on the nature of the evaluator. A secondary objective is to examine whether differences in self-presentation translate into different selection outcomes. The project explores a potential endogenous-input channel in AI-mediated evaluation, whereby the evaluation environment may affect not only how information is processed but also the information individuals choose to communicate.

“Empathy, Cognitive Reflection, and Social Preferences: Experimental Evidence from Distributional Choices”

Abstract / project description +

This project develops a new experimental design to study the causal interplay between empathy, cognitive reflection, and social preferences. Building on previous evidence linking perspective taking and decision time to prosocial choices, the project aims to move beyond observational associations by experimentally manipulating features of the decision environment that may activate empathic considerations or deliberative processing. The study focuses on how different components of empathy and cognitive reflection shape distributional choices and whether their effects depend on the salience of others' outcomes and on the cognitive demands of the decision. The broader objective is to identify mechanisms through which emotional and reflective processes jointly influence prosocial versus self-interested behavior.

“Evolving Rule of Law and Cooperation under Corruption: A Laboratory Experiment”

Abstract / project description +

This project develops a laboratory experiment to study how the evolution of institutional quality affects cooperation, bribery, punishment, and exit decisions in a public-goods environment. The proposed design assigns participants to the roles of citizens and monitors and varies the initial strength of the rule of law, contrasting institutional trajectories that either deteriorate or consolidate over time. Citizens may contribute to a public good, offer bribes, acquire information about the social environment, or opt out, while monitors can punish citizens but are themselves protected from punishment. The experiment is designed to identify how changes in institutional quality shape incentives for cooperative versus opportunistic behavior and to examine the conditions under which resilient institutions can emerge in corrupt environments.

“First Among Equals? Evidence on the Consequences of Author Ordering”

Abstract / project description +

This project explores whether conventions governing author ordering in academic publications affect career progression and recognition within disciplines. Building on data and research developed in the broader project on academic familism and university careers, the study considers how different authorship norms—including alphabetical, contribution-based, seniority-based, and inverse-seniority ordering—may shape the visibility and advancement of junior researchers. The project aims to investigate whether apparently neutral conventions generate systematic differences in academic outcomes and whether these effects vary across disciplines characterized by different authorship traditions.

“Supervised Calibration of LLM-Based Survey Simulation”

Abstract / project description +

This project investigates whether predictions from multiple large language models can be combined through a supervised calibration layer to improve the simulation of ordinal survey responses. Using pre-generated LLM responses to survey items on artificial intelligence and the future of work, the project explores alternative second-stage calibration strategies and compares them with individual LLM predictions and conventional machine-learning benchmarks. Particular attention is devoted to the potential trade-off between respondent-level predictive accuracy and the preservation of realistic response distributions and heterogeneity. The broader objective is to understand whether supervised calibration can improve the empirical usefulness of LLM-generated synthetic survey samples without excessively compressing the variation observed in real survey data.

“Attitudes toward AI in Europe: General Views, Work-Related and Regulatory Preferences in a Data-Driven Framework”

Abstract / project description +

This broader research project studies European citizens' perceptions and attitudes toward artificial intelligence using microdata from the Special Eurobarometer on Artificial Intelligence and the future of work. The project adopts a multidimensional perspective, distinguishing general evaluations of AI, perceived effects on employment and working conditions, attitudes toward its use in the workplace, regulatory preferences, and individual skills related to technological change. The empirical approach combines theory-driven econometric analysis with supervised and unsupervised machine-learning methods to investigate heterogeneity across individuals, social groups, and countries. The project has provided a broader empirical and conceptual framework from which more specific research strands have developed, including work on institutional trust, LLM-generated synthetic survey samples, and the role of digital skills in shaping attitudes toward AI.

“Market Formation and Resource Accessibility in Ancient Mesopotamia: An Integrated Archaeological and Economic Approach”

Abstract / project description +

This project investigates the emergence and spatial organization of economic systems and market networks in ancient Mesopotamia between 3000 and 1750 BCE. By integrating archaeological evidence with economic reasoning and spatial analysis, the project studies how access to natural resources and transportation costs may have shaped trade, settlement patterns, and institutional development. A central methodological component is the construction of realistic least-cost-path measures based on time-calibrated movement functions adapted to the Mesopotamian environment. These spatial tools are used to model accessibility, travel frictions, and potential exchange networks, providing an empirical basis for studying the location and hierarchy of markets and the economic mechanisms underlying early urbanization. The project is developed within the broader Origins research agenda.

“Intergenerational Social Mobility and Informal Ways to Find a Job”

Abstract / project description +

This project studies whether reliance on informal job-search channels contributes to the persistence of intergenerational inequality. Building on the idea that labour-market opportunities are partly embedded in social networks, the project investigates whether individuals who rely more heavily on family members, friends, or acquaintances to find employment are more likely to reproduce their parents' socio-economic position. The empirical strategy is designed around harmonized European labour-market microdata, including the Labour Force Survey and EU-SILC, and combines measures of intergenerational mobility with indicators of job-search methods and labour-market outcomes. The broader objective is to assess whether differences in the role of informal job-matching institutions across countries help explain cross-country variation in equality of opportunity.

“Look Up! A European Study on People's Thinking about Different Dimensions of Climate Change”

Abstract / project description +

This project investigates the multidimensional structure of European citizens' attitudes toward climate change using data from the European Social Survey. The analysis considers climate-change awareness, willingness to adopt environmentally responsible behaviors, preferences for the transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, trust in public and private initiatives addressing climate change, and support for related policies. The project combines fuzzy-logic methods to synthesize multiple attitudinal dimensions, the TOPSIS multi-criteria decision method to compare countries, and cluster analysis to identify broader patterns across European populations. The study provides a basis for examining how different dimensions of environmental concern combine at the individual and country levels.

“Preferences and Attitudes for Climate Change and Related Policies: Empirical Analysis Driven by Data and Theory in the European and Global Context”

Abstract / project description +

This broader research agenda investigates how socio-economic characteristics, individual values, preferences, trust, and social relationships shape perceptions of climate change, support for environmental policies, and political preferences related to the green transition. The project combines theory-driven empirical analysis with large-scale survey microdata and computational methods, including machine learning, to study heterogeneity across individuals and countries in both European and broader international contexts. It has provided the conceptual framework for several subsequent research outputs on environmental attitudes, political behavior, climate-related preferences, and responses to major socio-economic and geopolitical shocks.
